About

Background

I am a PhD candidate in Geological Engineering at the University of North Dakota, working with Dr. Taufique H. Mahmood in the Harold Hamm School of Geology and Geological Engineering. My dissertation examines hydrologic pathways and geochemical controls on nutrient and carbon export in cold-region agricultural watersheds of the Northern Great Plains.

The problem that keeps pulling me back is a mismatch of timescales. Nutrient export from these basins is episodic: runoff concentrates into a short melt season, the soil is frozen while it happens, and the flow paths that operate during those few days bear little resemblance to what runs in summer. Routine monitoring, which usually samples weekly to monthly, is unlikely to see any of it. Closing that gap between how the system behaves and how we observe it is most of what my field work is for.

I came to this through water resources engineering rather than through geochemistry. My master's work at BUET in Dhaka evaluated managed aquifer recharge systems in the southwestern coastal belt of Bangladesh, where the question was whether village-scale recharge schemes could supply drinking and irrigation water under salinity stress. Working on aquifers there and on frozen prairie streams here has made me pay close attention to where water actually goes, rather than where a model assumes it goes.

Alongside the field program I use the Cold Regions Hydrological Model, stable water isotopes, and remote sensing to connect what happens at a gauge to what happens across a watershed. A good part of my time also goes to running winter field campaigns on ice-covered streams, which is less glamorous and often more informative than it sounds.

Research interests: watershed hydrology · hydrogeology · water quality and nutrient transport · biogeochemistry · isotope geochemistry · groundwater and surface water interaction · cold-region hydrology · agricultural land management · remote sensing

Research

What I work on

Four connected problems in how nutrients and carbon leave cold agricultural watersheds, and why the conventional monitoring record has trouble answering them.

Method

Sampling the melt at the speed it moves

Monitoring agencies typically collect nutrient samples weekly, biweekly, or monthly, and concentrations and loads get estimated from that record. A prairie snowmelt hydrograph rises and falls in two to five days. A monthly sample has almost no chance of landing on the peak, and even a weekly one usually catches a limb rather than the event. I sample two to three times per day through the melt, which is the resolution needed to resolve the concentration–discharge relationship while the water is actually moving.

Regime comparison

Snow-dominated against rain-dominated runoff

Concentration–discharge behaviour is the standard tool for inferring where solutes come from and how supply limits export. How that behaviour differs between melt-driven and rain-driven runoff in cold agricultural basins is not well established, largely because few datasets have the temporal resolution to compare them fairly. Contrasting the two regimes within the same watersheds tests whether the source areas and flow paths that dominate in spring are the same ones that operate in summer.

Open question

Do phosphorus, nitrogen, and carbon respond alike?

These three are rarely measured together across contrasting runoff regimes in this setting, so whether they behave in parallel is still an open question. Organic carbon is the least predictable of them. Most cold-region carbon work comes from forested, peatland, and permafrost catchments, which commonly show flushing during melt. Cultivated watersheds lack the organic surface horizon that drives that response, so it is unclear whether the flushing signal survives cultivation at all.

Controls

Contributing area, frozen soil, and geochemical controls

Which conditions govern whether nutrients reach the stream during melt is understudied in prairie settings. Frozen ground, contributing area, redox state, ionic strength, and water temperature all plausibly matter, and they change quickly over a melt event. Pairing continuous hydrochemical measurement and stable water isotopes with the sub-daily nutrient record is how I try to separate the controls that carry real explanatory weight from those simply carried over from temperate catchments.
